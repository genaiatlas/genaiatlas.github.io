    Project Overview

    Business Context and Motivation

    This project addresses a fundamental challenge in modern e-commerce: helping customers discover relevant products within massive catalogs. Amazon and similar platforms invest heavily in recommendation systems because personalized suggestions directly impact user engagement, conversion rates, and revenue. Research indicates that recommendation engines can drive 20-35% of e-commerce revenue by reducing decision paralysis and surfacing products customers might not otherwise discover.

    Dataset Characteristics

    The Amazon Electronics product reviews dataset contains user-product interactions represented as ratings. The original dataset includes approximately 7.8 million ratings across various electronic products, with three key attributes:

    • user_id: Unique identifier for each customer
    • prod_id: Unique identifier for each product
    • rating: Numerical score from 1-5 indicating user satisfaction

    Critical Limitation: The dataset deliberately excludes product metadata (titles, descriptions, categories) and review text to ensure models rely purely on collaborative signals rather than content features. This design choice simulates scenarios where behavioral data is more readily available than rich content information.

    Methodological Approach

    This tutorial implements three progressively sophisticated recommendation strategies:

    Model 1: Rank-Based System - Recommends globally popular products based on aggregated ratings. This non-personalized baseline serves customers with no interaction history and provides a performance benchmark.

    Model 2: Collaborative Filtering - Leverages user-user and item-item similarity patterns to make personalized predictions. The assumption is that users who agreed in the past will agree in the future, and similar items receive similar ratings.

    Model 3: Matrix Factorization (SVD) - Decomposes the user-item rating matrix into latent feature representations, capturing underlying preference patterns more compactly than similarity-based methods.

    Each approach represents different trade-offs between personalization depth, computational requirements, and data sparsity handling.

    Data Preparation Strategy

    The Sparsity Challenge

    The original dataset's 7.8 million ratings might seem substantial, but e-commerce data exhibits extreme sparsity. Most users rate very few products, and most products receive very few ratings. This creates two problems:

    • Statistical Reliability: Users with 1-2 ratings provide insufficient signal for personalization
    • Cold Start: Products with minimal ratings cannot be confidently recommended

    Filtering Rationale

    The notebook implements a two-stage filtering process:

    User Filtering (50+ ratings threshold): Retains only engaged users who have demonstrated consistent platform usage. This ensures the model learns from reliable behavioral patterns rather than noise from casual browsers. The threshold of 50 ratings balances data retention with signal quality.

    Product Filtering (5+ ratings threshold): Keeps products with sufficient social proof. From a business perspective, recommending products with very few ratings carries reputational risk, as the rating may not represent true quality.

    Impact Assessment

    This filtering reduces the dataset to approximately 65,290 interactions (0.83% of original data), representing:

    • Users with genuine engagement patterns
    • Products with established market presence
    • Computationally manageable dataset size

    Trade-off Analysis: While this dramatically reduces data volume, it improves model reliability and computational efficiency. However, it creates a cold-start problem for new users and products, requiring separate strategies (content-based filtering, hybrid approaches) not covered in this tutorial.

    Business Implication: The filtered dataset represents Amazon's most valuable customer segment (highly engaged users) and most recommendable products (socially validated items), making it an appropriate focus for initial recommendation system deployment.

    Rank-Based Recommendations

    Methodology Overview

    Rank-based recommendation represents the simplest viable approach: identify globally popular products and recommend them to all users. The system calculates two metrics per product:

    • Average Rating: Mean of all user ratings for the product
    • Rating Count: Total number of ratings received

    Products are then ranked by average rating, with a minimum interaction threshold to ensure statistical reliability.

    Why This Approach Works (Sometimes)

    Appropriate Use Cases:

    • New User Onboarding: Users with no interaction history (cold start) receive reasonable initial suggestions

    • Explore vs. Exploit: Popular items provide safe "crowd-sourced" recommendations when exploration is valuable

    • Homepage Display: Generic landing pages benefit from universally appealing products

    Model Configuration and Results

    Configuration 1: 50 Minimum Interactions

    Recommends products with at least 50 ratings, ensuring reasonable statistical confidence in the average rating. This identifies well-established, highly-rated products.

    Configuration 2: 100 Minimum Interactions

    A more conservative threshold that prioritizes products with stronger social proof. This reduces the candidate set but increases confidence in product quality.

    Practical Interpretation: The products recommended under both thresholds represent "safe bets" - items that satisfy most customers. However, these recommendations are identical for all users, representing zero personalization.

    Limitations and Business Context

    Critical Weaknesses:

    • No Personalization: A user interested in headphones receives the same recommendations as someone shopping for cameras

    • Popular Item Bias: Recommends only mainstream products, failing to surface niche items that might perfectly match individual preferences

    • No Discovery: Users never encounter products outside the top-N most popular items

    • Feedback Loop Risk: Popular items become more popular, creating winner-take-all dynamics that may not reflect true quality

    When Rank-Based Is Sufficient: For businesses with limited computational resources, small catalogs, or primarily new users, this approach provides acceptable performance at minimal cost. However, e-commerce platforms like Amazon require personalization to maximize revenue and user satisfaction.

    Transition to Collaborative Filtering: The rank-based baseline establishes that basic aggregation provides some value. The question becomes: can we do better by considering individual user preferences? The collaborative filtering models address this question.

    Collaborative Filtering Systems

    Theoretical Foundation

    Collaborative filtering operates on a deceptively simple premise: users who agreed in the past will agree in the future. This manifests in two complementary approaches:

    User-User Similarity: If User A and User B rated many products similarly, and User A liked Product X, User B will likely also enjoy Product X.

    Item-Item Similarity: If Product X and Product Y receive similar ratings across users, a user who liked Product X will likely appreciate Product Y.

    Why Similarity-Based Methods Work

    The fundamental insight is that ratings encode latent preference signals. Two users who both rate action movies highly and romantic comedies lowly share underlying taste preferences, even if those preferences aren't explicitly articulated. Similarity metrics (cosine, Pearson correlation) quantify this alignment.

    User-User Collaborative Filtering

    Baseline Model Performance:

    The initial user-user model using cosine similarity achieves moderate performance on precision and recall metrics. These metrics answer critical business questions:

    • Precision@10: Of the 10 products recommended to each user, what fraction are actually relevant (rated ≥3.5)? This measures recommendation quality and impacts user trust.

    • Recall@10: Of all products a user would rate highly, what fraction appear in our top-10 recommendations? This measures coverage and opportunity cost of missed recommendations.

    • F1-Score: The harmonic mean balancing precision and recall, useful when both matter equally.

    Practical Interpretation: The baseline model provides better-than-random recommendations but has substantial room for improvement. Users receive some relevant suggestions, but many recommendations miss the mark.

    Hyperparameter Optimization

    The optimization process tunes three critical parameters:

    k (number of neighbors): How many similar users to consider when predicting ratings. Too few neighbors provides insufficient signal; too many introduces noise from dissimilar users.

    min_k (minimum neighbors): Fallback threshold ensuring predictions aren't based on too few similar users. When insufficient neighbors exist, the system defaults to global average rating.

    Similarity Metric: Choice between cosine, mean squared difference (MSD), or Pearson correlation affects how "similarity" is quantified.

    Optimization Results: Grid search cross-validation identifies optimal parameter combinations that improve RMSE (prediction accuracy) and precision/recall metrics. The improvements demonstrate that careful parameter tuning significantly impacts recommendation quality.

    Why This Matters: In production systems, even small metric improvements translate to substantial revenue gains. A 2% improvement in precision might mean millions of dollars in additional conversions for a platform like Amazon.

    Item-Item Collaborative Filtering

    Item-item filtering inverts the similarity calculation: instead of finding similar users, it finds similar products. This approach often outperforms user-user filtering in e-commerce contexts for several reasons:

    Product Stability: Product characteristics remain relatively stable, while user preferences evolve over time. Item similarity matrices can be precomputed and cached efficiently.

    Interpretability: "Customers who bought X also bought Y" is intuitively understandable and trustworthy for users.

    Scalability: With fewer products than users (typically), item-item similarity computations are more tractable.

    Performance Comparison: The optimized item-item model achieves comparable or superior performance to user-user models, with the additional benefit of computational efficiency.

    Practical Prediction Examples

    The notebook demonstrates predictions for specific user-product pairs:

    Observed Interaction: When predicting ratings for products users have already rated, the model's prediction should approximate the true rating. Discrepancies indicate model error or unusual rating patterns.

    Unobserved Interaction: For products users haven't rated, the prediction represents the system's best estimate. These predictions drive actual recommendations.

    Business Application: The get_recommendations() function identifies a user's top-N unobserved products by predicted rating. This ranked list drives the recommendation interface users see.

    Similarity-Based Limitations

    Despite improvements through optimization, similarity-based collaborative filtering has inherent constraints:

    Sparsity Struggles: When user-product interactions are sparse, finding sufficient similar users/items becomes difficult, leading to low-confidence predictions.

    Popularity Bias: Similarity metrics favor popular items with many ratings, potentially overlooking excellent niche products.

    Computational Cost: Real-time similarity computation for millions of users/products requires significant infrastructure.

    Cold Start: New users/products with no ratings cannot be effectively handled without auxiliary information.

    These limitations motivate the exploration of matrix factorization methods, which handle sparsity more elegantly.

    Matrix Factorization Approach

    Conceptual Shift from Similarity to Latent Factors

    Matrix factorization represents a fundamental paradigm shift in collaborative filtering. Instead of explicitly computing similarities, it learns latent feature representations that encode user preferences and product characteristics in a lower-dimensional space.

    Intuition: The rating matrix can be approximated as the product of two smaller matrices:

    • User Matrix: Each user represented by a vector of latent preferences (e.g., preference for action vs. drama, high-tech vs. simple features)

    • Product Matrix: Each product represented by a vector of latent characteristics

    The predicted rating is the dot product of the user and product vectors, capturing how well the product's characteristics align with the user's preferences.

    Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

    The SVD algorithm implemented via the Surprise library optimizes these latent factor matrices to minimize prediction error across observed ratings. The optimization process uses stochastic gradient descent (SGD) to iteratively adjust latent factors.

    Mathematical Elegance: SVD naturally handles the sparsity problem. Unlike similarity methods that struggle with missing values, SVD learns patterns from observed ratings and generalizes to unobserved interactions through the latent space geometry.

    Baseline SVD Performance

    The initial SVD model (with default hyperparameters) demonstrates strong performance, often exceeding optimized similarity-based models. This validates the theoretical advantage of latent factor models for sparse data.

    Key Metrics:

    • RMSE: Measures average prediction error in rating units. Lower values indicate more accurate predictions.

    • Precision@10 / Recall@10: Demonstrate that SVD recommendations are both accurate (high precision) and comprehensive (high recall).

    Why SVD Outperforms Similarity Methods:

    • Dimensionality Reduction: By projecting users and items into a shared latent space, SVD captures complex preference patterns that similarity metrics miss

    • Noise Robustness: Averaging over latent factors reduces impact of rating noise or outliers

    • Better Generalization: Learned latent representations generalize beyond observed similarities to predict novel user-item affinities

    Hyperparameter Tuning

    SVD's performance is sensitive to three key hyperparameters:

    n_epochs: Number of training iterations. More epochs allow better convergence but risk overfitting and increase training time.

    lr_all (learning rate): Controls how aggressively the algorithm updates parameters. Too high causes instability; too low slows convergence.

    reg_all (regularization): Penalizes model complexity to prevent overfitting. Balances fitting training data versus generalizing to test data.

    Optimization Process: Grid search evaluates all hyperparameter combinations via 3-fold cross-validation, selecting the configuration that minimizes RMSE. This process typically improves baseline performance by 3-10%, which translates to noticeably better recommendations.

    Prediction Interpretation

    When the optimized SVD model predicts ratings:

    For Observed Interactions: The prediction should closely match the true rating. Systematic deviations indicate model bias or that the user's rating pattern defies general trends.

    For Unobserved Interactions: The prediction represents a confidence-weighted estimate. High predicted ratings (e.g., 4.5+) suggest strong user-product affinity and warrant recommendation.

    Practical Application: The recommendations generated by SVD reflect not just explicit similarities, but deeper patterns in the latent preference space. This often surfaces unexpected but highly relevant suggestions that surprise and delight users.

    Matrix Factorization Limitations

    Despite superior performance, SVD has constraints:

    Interpretability Deficit: Latent factors lack clear semantic meaning. Unlike item-item filtering (which explains "similar products"), SVD recommendations are harder to justify to users.

    Training Requirements: SVD requires iterative optimization, making initial model training more computationally expensive than similarity computation.

    Cold Start Persistence: New users/products still require auxiliary approaches (content-based features, demographic data) to generate initial recommendations.

    Static Assumptions: The tutorial's SVD implementation treats ratings as static. Production systems must continually retrain or use online learning to capture evolving preferences.

    Model Comparison and Selection

    Performance Benchmark Summary

    Across the three modeling approaches, we observe a clear progression in recommendation quality:

    Rank-Based System:

    • Strengths: Simple, fast, no training required, reasonable for cold-start users
    • Weaknesses: Zero personalization, popularity bias, poor coverage
    • Use Case: Initial recommendations for brand-new users, homepage placeholders

    Collaborative Filtering (User-User & Item-Item):

    • Strengths: Personalized recommendations, interpretable similarity reasoning, no domain knowledge required
    • Weaknesses: Struggles with sparsity, computationally expensive at scale, cold-start limitations
    • Use Case: Mid-sized catalogs with moderate sparsity, scenarios requiring explainable recommendations

    Matrix Factorization (SVD):

    • Strengths: Best handling of sparse data, captures complex latent patterns, superior accuracy
    • Weaknesses: Less interpretable, requires model training, cold-start issues persist
    • Use Case: Large-scale production systems where prediction accuracy is paramount

    Evaluation Metric Considerations

    RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error): Measures prediction accuracy but doesn't directly reflect recommendation quality. A user cares whether recommended items are relevant, not whether predicted ratings are precise.

    Precision@k and Recall@k: More aligned with business objectives. High precision ensures users trust recommendations; high recall ensures users discover relevant products.

    F1-Score@k: Valuable when both precision and recall matter equally, providing a single metric for model comparison.

    Threshold Selection Impact: The 3.5 rating threshold for "relevant" items reflects the inflated rating distribution observed in EDA. Adjusting this threshold would alter precision/recall metrics but not fundamental model rankings.

    Production Deployment Considerations

    Real-world recommendation systems rarely deploy a single algorithm. Instead, they use ensemble approaches that combine multiple methods:

    Hybrid Architecture Example:

    • Rank-Based: Handles cold-start users and provides baseline recommendations
    • Item-Item CF: Generates "similar products" for product detail pages
    • SVD: Powers personalized homepage and email recommendations
    • Content-Based (not covered): Handles cold-start products using metadata
    • Multi-Armed Bandit (not covered): Balances exploration and exploitation in real-time

    Resource and Scalability Requirements

    Computational Costs:

    • Rank-based system: Negligible (simple aggregation)
    • Similarity-based CF: Moderate to high (similarity matrix computation and storage)
    • SVD: High initial training cost, low prediction cost once trained

    Latency Requirements: Production systems must return recommendations in milliseconds. Pre-computing similarity matrices or caching SVD predictions is essential for real-time performance.

    Update Frequency: As new ratings arrive, how frequently should models retrain?

    • Rank-based: Real-time aggregation updates feasible
    • Similarity-based: Batch updates (daily) common
    • SVD: Periodic retraining (weekly/monthly) with incremental learning for frequent updates

    Business Impact Assessment

    Measurable Outcomes: A/B testing would compare models on:

    • Click-through rate on recommendations
    • Conversion rate for recommended products
    • Revenue per user from recommended items
    • User engagement metrics (time on site, pages viewed)

    Expected Improvements: Literature suggests that collaborative filtering can improve conversion rates by 10-30% over non-personalized baselines, with matrix factorization providing an additional 5-15% gain over similarity-based methods.

    Remaining Challenges and Future Directions

    This tutorial addresses core collaborative filtering but omits several important considerations:

    Temporal Dynamics: User preferences evolve; recent interactions should weigh more heavily than old ones.

    Context-Aware Recommendations: Time of day, device type, browsing history provide valuable signals.

    Diversity and Serendipity: Optimizing solely for predicted rating creates filter bubbles. Injecting diversity improves user satisfaction.

    Cross-Domain Learning: Leveraging patterns across product categories improves cold-start handling.

    Deep Learning Approaches: Neural collaborative filtering and transformer-based models represent the current state-of-the-art but require substantially more data and compute.

    Final Recommendation

    For an e-commerce platform with Amazon's scale and resources:

    Implement a hybrid system that deploys rank-based recommendations for cold-start scenarios, item-item collaborative filtering for explainable "similar products" features, and SVD-based matrix factorization for personalized recommendations. Continuously evaluate model performance through A/B testing and iterate based on business metrics.

    For smaller platforms or learning contexts, start with item-item collaborative filtering after hyperparameter tuning. It provides a good balance of performance, interpretability, and implementation complexity.

    Evaluation Metric Function

    def precision_recall_at_k(model, k=10, threshold=3.5):
    """Return precision and recall at k metrics for each user"""

    user_est_true = defaultdict(list)
    predictions = model.test(testset)

    for uid, _, true_r, est, _ in predictions:
        user_est_true[uid].append((est, true_r))

    precisions = dict()
    recalls = dict()

    for uid, user_ratings in user_est_true.items():
        user_ratings.sort(key=lambda x: x[0], reverse=True)

        n_rel = sum((true_r >= threshold) for (_, true_r) in user_ratings)
        n_rec_k = sum((est >= threshold) for (est, _) in user_ratings[:k])
        n_rel_and_rec_k = sum(((true_r >= threshold) and (est >= threshold))
                              for (est, true_r) in user_ratings[:k])

        precisions[uid] = n_rel_and_rec_k / n_rec_k if n_rec_k != 0 else 0
        recalls[uid] = n_rel_and_rec_k / n_rel if n_rel != 0 else 0

    precision = round((sum(prec for prec in precisions.values()) / len(precisions)), 3)
    recall = round((sum(rec for rec in recalls.values()) / len(recalls)), 3)

    accuracy.rmse(predictions)

    print('Precision: ', precision)
    print('Recall: ', recall)
    print('F_1 score: ', round((2*precision*recall)/(precision+recall), 3))

    Purpose: Computes precision@k, recall@k, F1-score@k, and RMSE for recommendation model evaluation.

    Function Parameters:

    • model: Trained Surprise model to evaluate
    • k: Number of top predictions to consider (default 10)
    • threshold: Rating value above which items are considered "relevant" (default 3.5)

    Flow - Step by Step:

    Step 1: Generate Predictions 

    predictions = model.test(testset)
    Generates predicted ratings for all user-item pairs in the test set.

    Step 2: Organize Predictions by User 

    for uid, _, true_r, est, _ in predictions:
    user_est_true[uid].append((est, true_r))
    Groups predictions by user, storing (predicted_rating, true_rating) tuples.

    Step 3: Compute Per-User Metrics 

    user_ratings.sort(key=lambda x: x[0], reverse=True)
    Sorts each user's predictions by predicted rating (descending) to identify top-k recommendations.

    Step 4: Calculate Relevance Counts

    n_rel (relevant items): Count of items with true_rating ≥ threshold 

    n_rel = sum((true_r >= threshold) for (_, true_r) in user_ratings)

    n_rec_k (recommended items in top-k): Count of items with predicted_rating ≥ threshold in top-k 

    n_rec_k = sum((est >= threshold) for (est, _) in user_ratings[:k])

    n_rel_and_rec_k (relevant AND recommended): Count of items both relevant and in top-k recommendations 

    n_rel_and_rec_k = sum(((true_r >= threshold) and (est >= threshold))
                      for (est, true_r) in user_ratings[:k])

    Step 5: Compute Precision and Recall

    Precision@k Formula: 

    Precision@k = (relevant items in top-k) / (total items recommended in top-k)

    Recall@k Formula: 

    Recall@k = (relevant items in top-k) / (total relevant items)

    Zero-Division Handling: If n_rec_k or n_rel is 0, the respective metric is set to 0.

    Step 6: Aggregate Across Users 

    precision = round((sum(prec for prec in precisions.values()) / len(precisions)), 3)
recall = round((sum(rec for rec in recalls.values()) / len(recalls)), 3)
    Computes mean precision and recall across all users.

    Step 7: Compute F1-Score 

    F1 = 2 * (precision * recall) / (precision + recall)
    Harmonic mean of precision and recall, balancing both metrics.

    Mathematical Foundation:

    Precision@k measures recommendation accuracy: - High precision → most recommended items are relevant - Low precision → many irrelevant items recommended

    Recall@k measures recommendation coverage: - High recall → most relevant items are recommended - Low recall → many relevant items missed

    F1-score balances precision and recall: - Useful when both metrics matter equally - Harmonic mean (not arithmetic) penalizes imbalanced values

    Impact: This function provides comprehensive evaluation beyond simple RMSE, capturing recommendation quality from a business perspective.

    Matrix Factorization Implementation

    SVD Baseline Model

    svd = SVD(random_state=1, verbose=False)
svd.fit(trainset)

    Purpose: Trains a baseline matrix factorization model using Singular Value Decomposition.

    SVD Algorithm Overview:

    SVD decomposes the user-item rating matrix R (dimensions U × I) into three matrices:

    R ≈ P × Σ × Q^T

    Where: - P (U × f): User latent factor matrix - Σ (f × f): Diagonal matrix of singular values - Q (I × f): Item latent factor matrix - f: Number of latent factors (dimensionality of latent space)

    Prediction Formula:

    For user u and item i: 

    r̂_ui = μ + b_u + b_i + p_u · q_i

    Where: - μ: Global rating mean - b_u: User bias (tendency to rate higher/lower than average) - b_i: Item bias (tendency to receive higher/lower ratings) - p_u · q_i: Dot product of user and item latent factor vectors

    Training Process:

    Uses Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) to minimize: 

    Σ(r_ui - r̂_ui)² + λ(||p_u||² + ||q_i||² + b_u² + b_i²)

    This is sum of squared errors plus L2 regularization to prevent overfitting.

    Why SVD Works for Sparse Data:

    • Learns latent representations from observed ratings
    • Generalizes to unobserved entries through latent space geometry
    • Captures complex preference patterns in lower-dimensional space

    Default Hyperparameters:

    • n_factors: 100 (latent factor dimensionality)
    • n_epochs: 20 (training iterations)
    • lr_all: 0.005 (learning rate)
    • reg_all: 0.02 (regularization)

    Expected Performance: SVD typically outperforms similarity-based methods due to superior sparse data handling.

    Impact: Establishes matrix factorization baseline for comparison with optimized version.

    Evaluation Functions

    RMSE Computation

    RMSE (Root Mean Squared Error) measures the average magnitude of prediction errors:

    RMSE = √(Σ(r̂_ui - r_ui)² / N)

    Where: - r̂_ui: Predicted rating for user u, item i - r_ui: True rating - N: Number of predictions

    Interpretation:

    • RMSE in rating units (e.g., RMSE = 0.95 means average error of 0.95 rating points)
    • Lower is better
    • Squared errors penalize large deviations more than small ones

    Why RMSE Matters:

    • Standard metric for regression problems
    • Directly measures prediction accuracy
    • Enables comparison across different models

    Limitations:

    • Doesn't directly measure recommendation quality
    • Treats all prediction errors equally (but predicting 3 vs 4 may matter less than predicting 2 vs 5)
    • Can be dominated by a few large errors

    Precision@k Deep Dive

    Precision@k measures the fraction of recommended items that are relevant:

    Precision@k = |{recommended items that are relevant}| / k

    Business Interpretation:

    • High precision: Users see mostly relevant recommendations → trust system
    • Low precision: Users see many irrelevant recommendations → lose trust, stop clicking

    Example:

    If k=10 and a user rates 7 of the 10 recommended items ≥3.5, then Precision@10 = 0.7

    Trade-offs:

    • Easy to achieve high precision by only recommending "safe" popular items
    • But this sacrifices discovery and personalization
    • Balance precision with diversity and coverage

    Recall@k Deep Dive

    Recall@k measures the fraction of relevant items that are recommended:

    Recall@k = |{relevant items that are recommended}| / |{all relevant items}|

    Business Interpretation:

    • High recall: System surfaces most products user would like → maximizes opportunity
    • Low recall: Many products user would like are never shown → lost sales

    Example:

    If a user has 20 relevant items in the test set and 8 appear in top-10 recommendations, Recall@10 = 8/20 = 0.4

    Trade-offs:

    • Easy to achieve high recall by recommending many items (large k)
    • But this dilutes precision and overwhelms users
    • Must balance with precision

    F1-Score@k

    F1-Score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall:

    F1 = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)

    Why Harmonic Mean:

    Penalizes imbalanced values more than arithmetic mean:

    • If Precision=0.9, Recall=0.1: Arithmetic mean=0.5, Harmonic mean=0.18
    • Forces systems to balance both metrics

    When to Use F1:

    • When precision and recall are equally important
    • When you want a single metric summarizing recommendation quality
    • For comparing models when precision/recall trade-offs differ

    Limitations:

    • May not reflect business priorities if precision and recall have different costs
    • Threshold selection (e.g., 3.5) significantly impacts results

    Cross-Validation

    K-Fold Cross-Validation provides robust performance estimates:

    Process:

    1. Split data into K equal folds
    2. For each fold:
    3. Train on K-1 folds
    4. Test on remaining fold
    5. Average metrics across K folds

    Benefits:

    • Uses all data for both training and testing
    • Reduces variance in performance estimates
    • Detects overfitting (large train-test metric gap)

    In This Tutorial:

    GridSearchCV uses 3-fold cross-validation (cv=3), meaning each hyperparameter combination is evaluated on 3 different train-test splits, and the average RMSE is reported.
